First, the Course
What Is RSDM 5511?
Dental Materials: the 4-credit course every third-year student takes in the fall, Tuesdays 9:00 to 11:50 AM, July 7 through December 1. It has three parts, and the seminars you are here for are the third.
1 · Lectures
19 lectures across the first eleven weeks, from polymers to ceramics to digital manufacturing, three of them new for 2026.
2 · Adaptive Learning
5 modules where students work through cases at their own pace: 3 in class with clinical focus sessions, 2 asynchronous.
3 · BMCS
The BioMaterials Clinical Seminars: the last six Tuesdays of class, run by student groups, facilitated by you. The rest of this deck.
212 points in the course. The seminars touch 93 of them: 44 percent of every student’s grade.

BMCS BioMaterials Clinical Seminars
Course Evaluations, Every Year
“I want to learn about the new materials, not the old ones.”
That request became the BMCS. The 95 students are divided into eleven groups of 8 to 9, each with a faculty facilitator. Each group takes a material or technique that is new, innovative, controversial, or simply untested, investigates it with evidence-based literature, and teaches it to the class in a 70-minute seminar. Their findings become the class study guide, one quiz question, and two Exam #4 questions.
The students teach the class. You facilitate.

The Discussion Engine
Six Thinking Hats, six seats at the table
This is a critical thinking exercise: de Bono’s six modes of thinking, mapped onto the people who actually argue about a dental material. Wearing a hat forces students to see the same evidence through eyes that are not their own, the patient’s fears, the insurer’s math, the regulator’s standards, and that shift is where the learning happens. Dr. Pacheco learned it in a faculty learning community on critical thinking directed by Dr. Jauregui, so the expert is in this room.
Woven in, or at the end
Embed the hats through the presentation so classmates get pulled in as it goes, or close with them as a dedicated block. Either works; passive does not.
Be creative
An activity, a debate, a game, a recorded skit, a mock insurance denial, a patient interview. The thinking is the students’ job, but Claude or ChatGPT can help you generate ideas, prompts, and discussion starters for the topic.

Do the Homework
Evidence, and Where It Came From
Hold the groups to the highest level of evidence available. Every objective comes with a search strategy: databases, search strings, result counts, and the hierarchy level of what they found.
When evidence exists
Systematic reviews and clinical trials outrank case reports and bench data. Make them say which level they are standing on.
When it does not
The source of whatever information they used must be identified. An honest “nothing published yet” is a finding.
Contacting industry
Many of these topics lack peer-reviewed coverage. Teach them to reach manufacturers, bypass the sales reps, and get to the research teams for real answers.
Give them a head start
Start tonight: ask Claude or ChatGPT for candidate references on your topic, verify them in PubMed or Google Scholar, or hand it to Emily Harris. Then seed each objective with a starting stack: a review, two or three papers, even the manufacturer’s ad.

The Email Does the Organizing
You will receive a ready-to-send template. Fill in your topic, dates, and student names, modify anything you like, and send it today. It carries the whole structure so the group starts organized instead of waiting on you.
01
Purpose
Frames the course as “Defense Against the Dark Arts”: the critical thinking and research skills that protect them from marketing claims, scams, and half-truths for the rest of their careers.
02
Four roles to assign
Group leader coordinates and owns deadlines; researchers find the best 5 to 10 articles per objective; PPT creators and presenters build the 30 to 40 minute deck; case creators develop 2 to 3 clinical cases with photos from classmates, D4s, or faculty.
03
Eight dated steps
From assigning roles, to shortlisting articles, to your three meetings, to the near-final deck and written objective responses, to the final D2L post. You fill in the dates from your seminar date backwards.
Three notes the students need
Stay focused: it is a BioMaterials seminar, cover the material science before the clinical applications. Be organized: hit the internal deadlines so there is time for review. Make it engaging: build what you would want to sit through in someone else’s seminar.
Your fill-in block
Faculty facilitator, group number, topic, presentation date, first meeting date, drafts due, final due, and the student roster. Everything the group needs on one screen, and the reason they stop emailing you to ask.

Duty 02, In Practice
Write the Five Against These
The shape is fixed even when the topic changes: the first objective states the problem, the middle three carry the content of your topic, and the last one wraps everything into a decision tree applied to a clinical scenario. They are the source of the quiz question and the two exam questions, so write them as things a student can be tested on.
Written for a clinician, not for a grader.
01
State the problem
What issue is being addressed in a new or unconventional way?
02
Topic-specific
Compare, contrast, identify, classify, evaluate: whatever the topic demands.
03
Topic-specific
Claims vs. evidence usually lives here: what the ads promise against what the literature shows.
04
Topic-specific
And so do the fundamentals: do materials science principles support the claim?
05
Decision tree, applied
The wrap-up: build a decision tree from everything above and apply it to a clinical scenario on the topic.

A Model Set · Fall 2025Fluoride-releasing adhesives and white spot lesions
Five Objectives, Then Three Questions
State the problem, three objectives that carry the topic, then a decision tree applied to a clinical scenario. From those five you write three multiple-choice questions: one becomes the in-class quiz, two go on Exam #4.
BO 1
Identify the biological and clinical factors that contribute to white spot lesions around orthodontic brackets.
BO 2
Differentiate conventional bonding agents from fluoride-releasing adhesives by composition, mechanism of action, and clinical implications.
BO 3
Describe additional preventive strategies and analyze how they complement adhesive selection.
BO 4
Compare and contrast the advantages and disadvantages of fluoride-releasing adhesives and adjunctive methods using current evidence.
BO 5
Apply a clinical decision tree to select preventive measures for white spot lesion management based on patient risk factors and treatment context.
The quiz question objective 5 produced
A high-caries-risk patient in fixed appliances presents with early demineralization at the bracket margins and poor hygiene compliance. Which step comes first on the decision tree?
A. Debond and restoreB. Reinforce topical fluoride and hygiene before changing the adhesiveC. Replace every bracket with RMGID. Place sealant over the lesionsE. No change until debonding
Two more came from objectives 2 and 4 and went on Exam #4. Write the questions the objectives already answer, and the class studies the objectives to get them right.
